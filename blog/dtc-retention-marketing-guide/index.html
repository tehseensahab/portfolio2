Pillar guide

DTC retention marketing: the playbook for supplement and coffee brands

By Tehseen Arbab·Oct 23, 2026·11 min read

If you sell something people run out of, your growth ceiling is set by how many first-time buyers become second-time buyers. This guide covers the four levers that move that number, the metrics to track, and a 90-day order of operations I use with supplement and coffee brands.

Why retention matters more for consumables

Supplements and coffee share a property most ecommerce products lack: the customer will need to buy again. The only question is whether they buy from you, and when. Every bottle and every bag has a built-in expiry date, so the retention problem is largely a timing problem — reaching the customer shortly before they run out, with an easy way to reorder.

That is why a generic "30 days after purchase" email rarely works for these categories. A 30-capsule bottle and a 90-capsule bottle run out at very different times, and a coffee drinker finishes a 12 oz bag faster than a household that brews a couple of cups a week. The playbook below is built around that fact.

The four levers of retention

1. Replenishment timing

Calculate how long each product lasts, then send the reorder reminder before the customer runs out, not after. This single change usually does more than any amount of subject-line testing. The full method is in how to build a Klaviyo replenishment flow.

2. Subscriptions

A subscription turns a repeat purchase from a decision into a default. But subscriptions only help if they are flexible (skip, swap, change frequency) and if cancellations are handled thoughtfully. If you are choosing a platform, read Skio vs Recharge: how to choose; if you already have subscribers quietly leaving, start with how to build a cancel flow that saves subscribers.

3. Lifecycle flows and segmentation

Welcome, post-purchase education, replenishment, cross-sell, win-back and sunset: each stage has a job. Segment by behavior (product bought, order count, subscriber or not, engagement) rather than by purchase date alone. A quick way to find what is broken is a structured Klaviyo audit.

4. Measurement

Blended repeat rate hides everything. Track cohorts so you can see whether customers acquired this month behave better or worse than last quarter's. See how to read LTV and cohort retention curves.

The metrics that matter

MetricWhat it tells youHow to read it
Repeat purchase rateShare of customers who order againMeasure by cohort and by product, not just blended.
Time to second orderHow quickly customers returnCompare it with your product's days of supply.
Subscriber churnShare of subscribers cancelling per periodTrack by shipment number — early cancels point to expectations, late cancels to fatigue.
LTV : CAC and paybackWhether acquisition spend is sustainableUse gross margin, not revenue, when you can.
Retention revenue shareRevenue from returning customersA rising share relieves pressure on ad spend.

The retention stack

  • Klaviyo for email and SMS flows, segmentation and reporting.
  • Skio or Recharge for subscriptions, the customer portal and cancel flows.
  • Shopify as the source of order data and the checkout where subscriptions are sold.
  • Meta Ads for acquisition and retargeting that hands customers into the retention system instead of stopping at the first purchase.
  • A simple cohort spreadsheet or analytics tool to prove what is working.

A 90-day order of operations

  1. Days 1–14: audit and baseline. Review flows, segments, deliverability and subscription settings. Record baseline repeat rate, time to second order and subscriber churn.
  2. Days 15–40: fix the biggest leak. For most consumable brands that is replenishment timing and the post-purchase journey. Launch with an A/B test running from the start.
  3. Days 41–70: protect subscribers. Add flexible options (skip, swap, cadence), a cancel flow and a pre-renewal reminder.
  4. Days 71–90: segment, test and report. Build behavior-based segments, run the next round of tests and publish a cohort view so the gains are visible.

Common mistakes

  • Copying a template flow pack without checking how long the product actually lasts.
  • Offering discounts first. Discounts train customers to wait; timing and convenience usually cost less.
  • Counting a "saved" subscriber as a win without checking whether they cancel 60 days later.
  • Judging success by open rates instead of second orders and revenue per recipient.

What this looks like in practice

I led retention for a DTC supplement brand whose monthly revenue run-rate grew from roughly $500K to $700K, and helped a US coffee brand double revenue from $15K to $30K, using the same timing-first logic.
